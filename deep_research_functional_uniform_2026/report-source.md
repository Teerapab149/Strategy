# Market Attractiveness & Competitive Landscape

## Functional Uniform / Workwear / Medical Apparel B2B — Thailand & Southeast Asia

**Research dossier for People’s Garment Public Company Limited (PG)**  
**Cut-off date:** 2 September 2026  
**Purpose:** Evidence pack for incorporating into the Megu strategic-management report; this is not a replacement report.

> **Executive verdict — selectively attractive, not yet a proven large TAM.** Public evidence supports a recurring, specification-led institutional niche across healthcare, manufacturing, hospitality and government. It does not support a current, definition-consistent market-size or CAGR claim for the combined segment in Thailand or SEA. PG’s most defensible wedge is customized functional corporate/medical uniforms and selected reusable workwear, supported by fit-data, repeat-order service, branch-level fulfilment and product-performance proof. Commodity uniforms should be treated as price-led business; regulated surgical/isolation garments require a separate medical-device pathway.

## 1. Research boundary and evidence rules

### 1.1 Market definition used

This dossier separates four arenas because their economics and barriers are different:

- **Basic corporate/service uniform:** polos, shirts, blouses, trousers, skirts and ordinary uniforms used for identity and appearance.
- **Bespoke functional uniform:** corporate or medical apparel with comfort, stretch, moisture management, antibacterial, UV, visibility or similar performance claims, plus fitting and multi-location fulfilment.
- **Industrial protective workwear:** garments selected against a real workplace hazard. ISO 13688 is a general protective-clothing base and must be paired with hazard-specific standards such as ISO 20471 for high visibility or ISO 11612 for heat/flame protection. [S29–S31]
- **Medical apparel:** ordinary scrubs and nurse uniforms are not automatically medical devices. Surgical/isolation gowns or apparel marketed for protection against contamination may be medical devices, depending on intended use and national rules. [S26–S28, S32–S34]

Disposable nonwoven PPE, hospital linen and laundry services are discussed only where they affect competition or business-model economics; they are not treated as the same market as reusable uniforms.

### 1.2 Evidence hierarchy

**Fact** means government data, procurement documents, standards bodies or issuer filings. **Company disclosure** means a vendor’s official website or annual report and is described as such. **Analyst inference** is an interpretation derived from cited facts and never presented as measured market data. **NPD** means not publicly disclosed or no credible definition-consistent public estimate was located.

Every numerical statement is tied to a source. Commercial market-report snippets with opaque methodology are excluded from the market-size conclusion.

## 2. Market size and growth: what can and cannot be claimed

### 2.1 Defensible conclusion

**Thailand TAM/CAGR: NPD. SEA TAM/CAGR: NPD.** No authoritative public series was located that consistently combines corporate uniforms, industrial workwear, ordinary medical scrubs and regulated medical garments. Government and ASEAN sources measure broader apparel, technical textiles, healthcare, employment, tourism or project-level procurement. Those are useful demand proxies, but they cannot be added together or converted into a market value without assumptions that the sources do not disclose. [S01–S04, S17–S19]

Two figures that may appear attractive but should not be used as the target-market size are:

- A Thailand Textile Institute article relays an Asia-Pacific medical-textiles outlook of USD 3 billion and 8.3% CAGR for 2020–2027. Its scope is broad medical textiles and the geography is Asia-Pacific, not Thailand B2B medical apparel. [S03]
- Thailand’s 2018 medical-technical-textile exports were USD 59.86 million, up 7%, but the cited leading products were bandages/gauze, cellulose hygiene products and sutures—not uniforms or scrubs. [S04]

The correct report language is therefore: “No current public estimate with a matching definition was found; attractiveness is assessed from demand-base, procurement and competitor-economics evidence.”

### 3.2 Geography prioritization — analyst inference

**Thailand — first priority.** PG already reports corporate/workwear clients in finance, government, state enterprises, private companies and education, and it expanded medical uniforms in 2025. Thailand also offers a visible hospital/factory demand base and procurement evidence. The constraint is strong domestic rivalry and lack of PG segment economics. [S05–S16, S35]

**Singapore — selective, tender-led.** GeBIZ provides transparent multi-year opportunities with firm and option periods, sample approval, embroidery and schedule-of-rates structures. Foreign suppliers can register, but individual opportunities may require Government Supplier Registration and financial standing. A local service partner is likely important for fitting and fulfilment. [S20–S23, S45]

**Indonesia — partner-led scale option.** Manufacturing and healthcare investment are attractive directional signals, but Trisula is an integrated incumbent and public e-procurement/local delivery requirements create access friction. [S18–S19, S39, S46]

**Malaysia — opportunity with local-access constraints.** A 2026 Hospital Kuala Lumpur tender illustrates that some opportunities can be restricted to Bumiputera firms registered with the Ministry of Finance under a specified field code. Medical PPE requirements are also explicit. Local partnering and SKU classification are prerequisites. [S24, S27, S33]

**Vietnam and Philippines — selective account development.** Both have national e-procurement and medical-device pathways. Public examples show short-cycle lump-sum orders as well as Class A gown registration, but no evidence supports an average contract size or cadence. [S25, S28, S46]

### 4.2 What is known about repeat orders

Racé publicly states that it retains each wearer’s size/pattern for the life of a contract, offers onsite/mobile fitting, individual or branch-level sorting and delivery, and can reproduce garments immediately. These claims are not independent market averages, but they reveal the service standard a serious competitor markets to enterprise buyers. [S35–S36]

The strongest evidence-based interpretation is that switching friction is created by approved fabric and patterns, wearer-level size data, embroidery/brand files, quality acceptance and fulfilment routines. Replacement and new-employee orders can then be easier for the incumbent. However, **average reorder interval, renewal rate, churn, backlog and average contract value remain NPD** for PG and the Thai competitors reviewed.

### 4.3 Buyer power and cash conversion

Public tenders can use price criteria, require e-GP registration, financial capacity, performance security and defect correction. The BMA case required 5% contract security and remediation within seven days; applicable preferences for SMEs or Made in Thailand depend on current rules and tender conditions. [S13, S43–S44]

A transparent overseas corporate-uniform analogue reports 1–3 year price validity and typically 60-day customer credit, but it is a Hong Kong issuer—not proof of Thai or SEA averages. It should be used only as a working-capital risk analogue. [S50]

## 5. Margin and value drivers

### 5.1 What the public numbers support

There is **no reliable public industry gross-margin benchmark** for the exact Thailand/SEA segment. PG’s 2025 company-wide gross margin was 21.40%, but the company does not disclose a uniform/medical segment margin. Thai Wacoal, TNLX, PJ Garment, FN and BTNC also do not publish comparable uniform-only margins. [S12, S35–S38]

The closest direct regional comparator is Trisula. Its 2024 uniform segment reported IDR 168.079 billion of sales, IDR 128.654 billion cost of sales and IDR 39.425 billion gross profit. The derived gross margin is 23.46%, versus 19.55% in 2023. Sales fell 2.99% while gross profit rose 16.38%. This demonstrates that mix, pricing, input cost and execution can matter more than volume, but 23.46% is **not a market average and not a PG forecast**. [S39]

Medtecs adds a different lesson: in 2024 its hospital-services gross profit rose after contract renewals lifted linen rates and consumption increased, while the company still recorded a large net loss partly associated with slow-moving PPE inventory provisions and expected credit loss. Service contracts can improve economics, but inventory and credit risk can overwhelm them. [S40]

### 5.2 Evidence-supported value drivers

- **Performance proof:** hazard or use-case standards, wash durability, opacity, mobility, antibacterial or moisture functions must be demonstrable—not marketing adjectives. [S15, S29–S34]
- **Fit and wearer data:** onsite measurement, mobile fitting, controlled size range, stored patterns and alteration/remake service reduce adoption friction. [S13, S35–S36]
- **Repeat-order speed:** stable approved fabric, patterns, trims, embroidery files and size records shorten replenishment and new-hire fulfilment. [S35–S36]
- **Multi-location fulfilment:** individual labelling, person/branch sorting and delivery solve procurement workload for buyers. [S36]
- **Life-cycle cost:** industrial laundering performance, replacement rate and loss control can matter more than purchase price. ISO 15797 provides a test basis for industrial-laundering effects; service competitors also use rental/laundry/RFID models. [S29, S41]
- **Traceability and sustainable procurement:** GRS chain of custody, Green Label, CoolMode, energy labels and Made in Thailand can differentiate where the TOR or buyer policy values them, but they are not universal legal requirements. [S11, S35, S43–S44]
- **References and delivery reliability:** named institutional references, sample approval and multi-year option continuity create credibility barriers. [S20–S24, S35, S39]

### 5.3 Cost and margin drags

Custom SKUs, small and frequent lots, size complexity, samples, onsite fitting, alterations, individual packaging, branch delivery, warranty/remake, testing, tender preparation, performance security, long credit and safety stock all consume contribution margin and working capital. Large-volume orders can improve utilization, but PG itself says it collaborates on cost management for high-volume orders, indicating buyer power can offset scale benefit. [S12–S13, S35–S36, S50]

PG should calculate contract contribution margin rather than apply a market-average markup:

> **Contract contribution = invoiced revenue − fabric/trim − direct labour − subcontract testing/sterilization − sampling/fitting − alterations/remakes − individual/branch fulfilment − warranty/defect cost − tender cost − financing and inventory carrying cost.**

No numeric premium should be assigned to antibacterial, moisture-management, flame-resistant, recycled or other features until quote-to-order and customer/SKU contribution data prove it.

### 6.3 Competitive structure

**Analyst inference:** rivalry is bifurcated. Basic polos and office uniforms are fragmented, price-led and accessible to many suppliers. Enterprise bespoke and functional contracts have fewer credible contenders because the supplier must coordinate design approval, samples, wearer data, multi-size production, QC, branch fulfilment and repeat orders. Protective workwear and regulated medical apparel add product-testing, traceability, QMS and registration barriers. [S13–S16, S20–S39]

PG’s disclosed strengths are integrated design/knitting/garment production, a 222-supplier pool, total capacity of 2.712 million pieces and 83.68% utilization in 2025, ISO 9001/14001 and GRS, and an existing corporate/workwear base. The capacity and margins are company-wide, not uniform-specific. [S12]

PG’s evidence gaps relative to leading rivals are more important than its sewing capacity: no named reference clients; no uniform/medical revenue or margin; no bid-win rate, backlog, contract length or reorder rate; no published sample lead time, OTIF, alteration/return rate or employee-level ordering/size system; and no current product-level medical-PPE or workwear certificate scope. These should be treated as diligence items, not assumed weaknesses in actual operations.

## 7. Barriers to entry

### 7.1 Commercial and operational barriers

1. **Approved pattern, colour and fit system.** Samples, wearer measurements and retained patterns create implementation work that a new supplier must repeat. [S13, S20, S35–S36]
2. **Multi-size, small-lot and replenishment flexibility.** New hires and replacements create small, frequent orders even when the initial rollout is large. Flexible inventory and production scheduling are required; frequency is NPD. [S35–S36, S50]
3. **Service footprint.** Onsite fitting, alterations, individual labelling and multi-branch delivery require local operations or partners. [S35–S36]
4. **References and incumbent credibility.** Publicly listed accounts and prior tender performance reduce buyer risk; current contract status may not be public. [S35, S39]
5. **Tender administration and financial capacity.** Vendor registration, e-procurement, bid documents, performance security, financial standing and country-specific eligibility can exclude otherwise capable manufacturers. [S13, S24, S43–S46]
6. **Working capital.** Multi-year price commitments, safety stock, option periods and credit terms can create inventory and receivable risk. [S20–S22, S40, S50]
7. **Functional and regulatory proof.** Test reports must match the hazard and intended use; ISO 13688 alone is insufficient for a protective claim. Medical claims can trigger national device regulation. [S26–S34]

### 7.2 Barrier intensity by arena

| Arena | Entry barrier | Main exclusion mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Basic corporate garments | Low–medium | Price, design acceptance, delivery and relationships. |
| Enterprise bespoke uniforms | Medium–high | References, wearer-data process, approved patterns, QC, fulfilment and continuity. |
| Industrial protective workwear | High for safety-critical use | Hazard-specific standard, test evidence, traceability and buyer audit. |
| Ordinary scrubs / nurse uniforms | Medium | Fabric opacity/comfort/wash durability, fit, hospital experience and fulfilment. |
| Surgical/isolation gowns | High | Medical-device classification/registration, QMS, barrier/sterility requirements and specialist manufacturing. |

## 8. Certification and regulatory requirements

### 8.1 Decision path

**Step 1 — Define intended use and claims.** If the garment is only an ordinary corporate uniform or comfort scrub, do not market it as PPE or a medical device. If it claims protection against contamination, blood-borne pathogens, heat, flame, low visibility, chemicals or other hazards, the relevant regulatory and technical pathway changes. [S26–S34]

**Step 2 — Identify the country and buyer TOR.** The ASEAN Medical Device Directive provides a regional framework, but registration is national. Public buyers can add their own standards, labels, local-content and vendor-registration requirements. [S26, S43–S46]

**Step 3 — Build a SKU-level evidence pack.** Test reports, QMS certificates, material certificates, traceability, labelling and declarations must refer to the specific product and valid scope. A corporate-level ISO certificate does not prove a garment meets a hazard standard.

### 8.2 Requirement map

| Use case | Relevant requirement / standard | Status and caution |
|---|---|---|
| General protective clothing | ISO 13688:2013 | Covers ergonomics, innocuousness, sizing, ageing, compatibility, marking and information. Must be used with a specific protective standard. [S29] |
| High-visibility clothing | ISO 20471:2013 | Covers colour, retroreflection, minimum areas and placement. Use when the hazard/TOR requires visibility. [S30] |
| Heat and flame | ISO 11612:2015 | Performance requirements for clothing protecting against heat/flame. Select only for relevant hazards. [S31] |
| Industrial-laundering durability | ISO 15797:2017 | Test basis for dimensional stability, colour, seam/crease appearance and related effects of industrial laundering. Actual laundry conditions should also be validated. [S29] |
| Thailand surgical/isolation gown | Thai FDA listing and 2020 specific standard notice | Thai FDA lists surgical/isolation gowns and coveralls as Class 1 listing devices; intended use matters. Lab testing may cover flammability, lint, liquid penetration, tensile and hydrostatic resistance. [S32] |
| Thailand device manufacturing QMS | Thai FDA GMP framework | From 3 July 2024, Class 1 manufacturers face Annex C minimum requirements; Classes 2–4 use GMP Annex A, ISO 13485:2016 or TIS 13485-2562, subject to transition provisions. Apply only after SKU classification. [S32] |
| Malaysia medical PPE | MDA/GD/0058 | Non-surgical gowns: PB70 Level 1/2 or EN 13795; surgical/isolation gowns: PB70 Level 3 or recommended Level 4 or EN 13795; sterile products also EN 556 or equivalent, plus labelling. [S27] |
| Singapore medical gown | HSA intended-purpose and Class A pathway | HSA’s Class A guide lists surgical gowns under Rule 4; dealer licensing applies even where Class A product registration is exempt. Confirm each product. [S28] |
| Philippines medical gown | FDA CMDN | Class A devices use Certificate of Medical Device Notification; FDA verification includes surgical gown examples. [S28] |
| Regional medical-device framework | ASEAN Medical Device Directive | Four risk classes, essential safety/performance, substantiated claims, labelling, traceability and national registration/authorized representative. [S26] |
| Recycled-content claim | Global Recycled Standard | Product/chain-of-custody and transaction-certificate requirements; GRS product claims require the applicable recycled-content threshold and other environmental/social/chemical controls. [S11] |
| Harmful-substance assurance | OEKO-TEX Standard 100 | Independent testing/certification for harmful substances across textile and accessory stages. Verify certificate, class and scope. [S11] |
| Thailand green/local procurement | Green Label, CoolMode, Label No.5, Made in Thailand | Voluntary or tender/policy-specific differentiators, not blanket legal mandates. Check current certificate and TOR. [S35, S43–S44] |

## 10. Strategic implications for PG

### 10.1 Recommended market position

PG should compete as a **functional-uniform program operator**, not only a garment factory. The offer should combine approved fabric/function, design, individual size data, sampling, multi-location rollout, rapid replenishment, alteration/remake controls and auditable performance evidence.

The priority order supported by evidence is:

1. **Thailand bespoke corporate/workwear and ordinary medical scrubs** for accounts where PG already has channel access and can prove service-level execution.
2. **Selected industrial functional workwear** one hazard and one standard at a time, beginning with use cases where PG or its fabric partners already possess test capability.
3. **Singapore/SEA non-device uniforms via local partners and tenders,** using Thailand as the production base and local teams for fitting, registration and delivery.
4. **Protective medical apparel only as a separate option,** after product classification, QMS gap assessment, test plan, sterilization/supply-chain design and customer economics are complete.

### 10.2 Do not claim yet

- “The Thailand/SEA market is worth X” or “growing Y%” for the combined segment.
- “Functional uniforms have higher industry margins.”
- “Contracts renew annually” or any universal reorder interval.
- “All medical uniforms are medical devices” or “ISO 13688 certification alone proves protection.”
- Current exclusive relationships with customer logos listed by competitors.
- Uniform-segment economics derived from PG, Thai Wacoal, BTNC, FN or another company’s total margin.

### 10.3 90-day evidence plan before committing capital

| Workstream | Minimum evidence | Decision test |
|---|---|---|
| Internal segment P&L | Revenue, gross profit and contribution by customer/SKU; fabric, labour, sample, alteration, branch-delivery, warranty and financing costs. | Identify contracts that create cash contribution after service cost, not merely factory utilization. |
| Commercial funnel | Named pipeline, tender count/value, bid/no-bid, win rate, reasons lost, sales cycle, option periods and expected call-off. | Demonstrate repeatable acquisition in at least two target verticals. |
| Reorder economics | Reorder share, months to first reorder, new-hire vs replacement mix, minimum economic batch, obsolete fabric/trim rate. | Prove incumbent advantage and working-capital burden. |
| Service operations | Sample lead time, first-pass approval, OTIF, measurement error, alteration/remake rate, branch-delivery accuracy and complaint closure. | Match or exceed Racé-style service benchmark. |
| Certification map | Intended use and claims by SKU; applicable law/TOR; current test reports and certificate scope; partner gaps. | No protective or medical claim without a complete evidence path. |
| SEA route to market | Country-specific partner, vendor registration, import/authorized-representative pathway, fitting/delivery model and tender shortlist. | Proceed country-by-country only where access cost and local service economics are viable. |

## 11. Final market-attractiveness conclusion

The opportunity is credible because demand pools are institutional and recurring, buyers publish detailed specifications and multi-year options, and high-service incumbents invest in fit, data and fulfilment. It is not yet proven as a large or fast-growing market in monetary terms because the exact Thailand/SEA TAM, CAGR, market share and average contract economics are not publicly disclosed.

For PG, the strongest risk-adjusted thesis is not “medical apparel is a high-margin growth market.” It is: **PG may be able to convert existing apparel capabilities into sticky, repeatable institutional programs where product function, fit-data and fulfilment reduce the buyer’s total operational burden.** That thesis becomes investable only after PG supplies customer/SKU contribution margins, pipeline conversion, reorder behavior, service KPIs and certification gaps.
